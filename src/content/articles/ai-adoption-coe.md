## The gap between "we deployed Copilot" and "we use it"

I've watched this pattern repeat across multiple multi-site rollouts: a company deploys a generative AI tool org-wide, employees try it for a week, and usage flattens out at "help me write an email." The tool works. The literacy training happened. What's missing is the bridge between generic AI capability and a specific business process.

The fix isn't more training. It's a **home-market use-case library** — packaging a handful of proven, business-process-embedded use cases from wherever the technology matured first, and treating every new site's rollout as a *localization* exercise rather than a from-scratch invention exercise.

## The three-tier structure that made it repeatable

1. **HQ as strategic owner** — sets the platform, standardizes training, curates the use-case library, allocates budget.
2. **Site representatives** — selected against an explicit filter: skill-verified literacy, tool-license eligibility, and cross-departmental role fit. Not seniority. Not availability.
3. **A facilitation partner** — runs the localization workshops, but explicitly does not own the AI platform or the business process itself.

Removing the ambiguity in that last point mattered more than I expected. When a delivery partner starts feeling responsible for "AI adoption" as an abstract goal, engagements drift into generic training. When their mandate is narrowly "customize this specific use case for this specific site," Quick Wins actually ship.

## Train everyone, implement with a few

The single biggest adoption-fairness risk in a phased rollout is perceived favoritism — three sites get hands-on workshops, twenty don't. The mitigation that worked: run remote AI-basics training simultaneously for every eligible site, while reserving intensive on-site customization for a deliberately small pilot cohort. Everyone gets something in week one. Only the pilot sites get the deep dive, and everyone understands why.

## Customization needs a checklist, not intuition

Every proven use case needs to be re-localized across the same six dimensions every time: business process fit, data/reference sources, regulatory or tax rules, language and output format, access and security, and the operating KPI. Skipping the checklist and "just letting the local team figure it out" works for the first site and collapses by the third — nobody remembers which dimension broke last time.

## Quick Wins need a measurement date before the workshop ends

A pilot use case without a pre-agreed KPI and a measurement date is not a Quick Win — it's a demo that nobody will revisit. Before any on-site workshop concludes, the plan needs: the To-Be process, the operating rules, an issue tracker, and a specific date (commonly four weeks out) when someone checks the number against the target and decides continue, adjust, or stop.

## What I'd tell a team starting this today

Resist the urge to bundle "build a net-new AI agent" into a Quick Win pilot. Deep technical customization — RAG, live system integration — is real, valuable work, but it belongs in a clearly-labeled *later* phase. Committing to it inside a fast pilot quietly turns a low-risk adoption exercise into a slow platform build, and you lose the only thing that justified running a pilot in the first place: speed.
